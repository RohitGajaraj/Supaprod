## The brief

## Startup Strategy Reset: Validate the Problem Before We Build Further

I want us to take a serious step back from what we have been building over the last ~3 months.

**Do not assume that the current product, direction, architecture, or idea is correct.** I want you to independently evaluate whether we are actually building something that customers need and whether there is a meaningful business opportunity here.

I am increasingly concerned that we have been continuously building without stepping back enough to ask whether we are building the **right thing**.

### 1. Start with first principles

Before suggesting features, improvements, architecture, or further development, answer:

* What problem are we actually trying to solve?
* Who has this problem?
* How painful and frequent is the problem?
* How are people solving it today?
* Why is the existing solution insufficient?
* Is this a "nice to have" or a genuinely important problem?
* Is there evidence that customers would pay for a solution?
* Are we solving a problem that is becoming more important, or one that may disappear as the market evolves?
* What assumptions are we currently making that may be wrong?

I want you to distinguish clearly between **facts, assumptions, hypotheses, and speculation**.

### 2. Be extremely skeptical of our current direction

Do not try to validate my existing thinking simply because we have already invested three months into it.

I want you to actively look for reasons why we may be wrong.

Ask:

* Are we solving the wrong problem?
* Are we targeting the wrong customer?
* Is the market too small?
* Is the problem not painful enough?
* Is the timing wrong?
* Is the market already becoming commoditized?
* Are major platform/model providers likely to absorb this functionality?
* Is there a much simpler way to solve the problem?
* Are there adjacent problems that are substantially more valuable?
* Are we building something technically interesting but commercially weak?
* Are we creating a product that users might like but not actually pay for?
* Are there fundamental reasons this startup should not exist?

**If you conclude that we are wrong, say so directly. Do not protect the idea or our previous work.**

I would rather discover now that we should pivot or stop than spend another 6–12 months building the wrong thing.

### 3. Evaluate the market from the outside

Conduct fresh market research rather than relying primarily on our existing assumptions.

Look at:

* Current startup activity
* Emerging categories
* Customer pain points
* Developer/enterprise pain points
* Funding trends
* Acquisition activity
* Major technology shifts
* New platform capabilities
* Open-source developments
* AI/agentic-system adoption
* Infrastructure opportunities
* Horizontal opportunities
* Vertical opportunities
* Developer tooling
* Enterprise software
* Agent infrastructure
* Agent orchestration
* Agent reliability/evaluation
* Agent security
* Agent memory/context
* Agent observability
* Agent deployment
* Agent-to-agent communication
* Human-agent workflows
* Data/connectivity layers
* Any other emerging categories where there appears to be substantial unmet demand

Do not limit the research to these categories. Explore wherever the evidence leads.

### 4. Identify the real opportunities

I want you to search broadly for **problems rather than products**.

Look for areas where:

1. Customers have a significant unresolved problem.
2. Existing solutions are inadequate.
3. New technology makes a substantially better solution possible.
4. The market is large or rapidly growing.
5. There is room for a startup to build a defensible business.
6. The opportunity is not simply a feature that OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, AWS, etc. can easily absorb.
7. There is a realistic path from an MVP to a meaningful company.

Identify both:

* Opportunities directly adjacent to our current product
* Completely different opportunities where our existing knowledge, technology, or work could give us an advantage

### 5. Consider whether we should pivot — or stop

I want you to explicitly evaluate four possibilities:

**A. Continue:**
The fundamental thesis is sound and we should continue, perhaps with specific changes.

**B. Narrow:**
The underlying opportunity is valid, but we are trying to solve too broad a problem.

**C. Pivot:**
There is a better problem/customer/market adjacent to what we are doing, and we should redirect the company.

**D. Stop and restart:**
The evidence suggests that continuing this product is unlikely to produce a strong company, and our time would be better spent pursuing a different opportunity.

Do not assign arbitrary scores or rankings. Instead, explain the evidence and reasoning behind each scenario.

### 6. Examine our YC rejection realistically

We recently received a rejection from Y Combinator and were not selected.

I do **not** want you to automatically interpret this as proof that the startup is bad. But I also don't want you to dismiss it.

Treat the rejection as one data point.

Investigate what can reasonably be inferred from it and what cannot.

Ask:

* Does the rejection reveal anything about how compelling the current thesis may be?
* Are there common characteristics among startups in this space that YC and other investors are currently looking for?
* Is our positioning weak?
* Is the problem insufficiently compelling?
* Is the market opportunity unclear?
* Is there insufficient evidence of customer demand?
* Are we simply too early?
* Or is there a deeper problem with the thesis?

Do not make unsupported assumptions about why YC rejected us.

### 7. Explore the agentic AI landscape deeply

I want a particularly deep analysis of the emerging **agentic systems** ecosystem.

Map the stack from infrastructure to applications.

For example:

**Infrastructure → Models → Agent runtimes → Tools/connectivity → Memory/context → Orchestration → Observability/evaluation → Security → Deployment → Applications/verticals**

But don't assume this framework is correct. Improve it if necessary.

Identify where the most meaningful problems appear to exist.

For each promising area, investigate:

* Who experiences the problem?
* Why does it exist?
* Why hasn't it already been solved?
* What are people currently using?
* What is changing because of increasingly capable models?
* What becomes possible now that wasn't possible 12–24 months ago?
* What becomes obsolete?
* Where is there infrastructure demand?
* Where is there application demand?
* Where could a startup establish a defensible position?
* Where are incumbents likely to dominate?
* Where are developers/enterprises currently struggling?

### 8. Evaluate the new "JEV" model/technology

There is also a new model/technology called **JEV** that appears to have capabilities around decision-making and could potentially be relevant to our existing product.

However, **do not start by figuring out how to integrate JEV into our existing product.**

First determine whether our existing product should exist.

Only after validating the underlying problem should you investigate:

* What JEV actually does
* Its capabilities and limitations
* What new products it enables
* Whether it changes the economics or feasibility of our idea
* Whether it makes our current product more valuable
* Whether it makes our current product obsolete
* Whether it enables a completely different product
* Whether competitors could use it more effectively than we could
* Whether it creates an entirely new startup opportunity

Treat JEV as a potential technological enabler, **not as a reason to preserve our current product direction.**

### 9. Research requirements

Do the necessary external research.

Use current, credible sources wherever possible, including:

* Startup/funding data
* Customer discussions
* Developer communities
* Product launches
* Technical research
* Industry reports
* Competitor websites
* Pricing information
* GitHub/open-source activity
* Job postings where relevant
* Investor theses
* Founder discussions
* Market data
* Relevant Reddit/community discussions where useful

Prioritize primary sources and recent information.

Clearly distinguish evidence from interpretation.

Do not cherry-pick evidence that supports our current thesis.

### 10. Generate alternative directions

After the research, identify the strongest **problem spaces** we could potentially pursue.

For each one, explain:

* Problem
* Target customer
* Existing alternatives
* Why existing solutions are insufficient
* Why now?
* Technology tailwind
* Market opportunity
* Potential business model
* Competitive landscape
* Defensibility
* Distribution challenge
* MVP that could validate the hypothesis quickly
* What evidence we would need before investing heavily
* Major reasons the idea could fail

Include opportunities that are **not related to our current product** if the evidence suggests we should consider them.

### 11. Design a validation plan

Most importantly, don't let this become another research exercise where we spend weeks thinking and then return to building.

Create a practical validation process.

Tell me:

* What are the 5–10 most important assumptions we need to validate?
* Which assumptions are existential?
* What can we test within 48 hours?
* What can we test within one week?
* What customer conversations should we have?
* What should we ask them?
* What behavior should we measure?
* What would constitute strong evidence?
* What would constitute weak evidence?
* What evidence would cause us to kill the idea?
* What evidence would justify continuing?

The objective should be to **maximize learning while minimizing additional engineering investment.**

### 12. Final recommendation

At the end, I want you to give me a clear strategic assessment.

Not a generic "there is potential" answer.

Tell me:

1. What do you believe we currently understand correctly?
2. What are we probably misunderstanding?
3. What assumptions are most dangerous?
4. What evidence is currently missing?
5. What should we stop doing immediately?
6. What should we continue doing?
7. What should we investigate next?
8. Should we continue, narrow, pivot, or seriously consider starting something else?
9. What is the fastest way to determine whether that conclusion is correct?

**Be intellectually honest and adversarial toward our assumptions.**

The goal is not to make me feel good about the work we have already done.

The goal is to determine whether we are building something that genuinely deserves to exist, whether there is a large and urgent customer problem behind it, and where we should invest the next 6–12 months of our time.

If the answer is "we are building the wrong thing," I want you to say that clearly.

If the answer is "there is something valuable here, but we are approaching it incorrectly," explain exactly what needs to change.

And if you discover a substantially better opportunity elsewhere, I want you to surface it even if it means abandoning everything we have built so far.

**Start from first principles. Research the market. Challenge our assumptions. Then tell me what the evidence suggests we should do next.**
