## Part 2: About the company

**2a. The problem you are trying to solve and your proposed solution**

```
Engineers got agents and shipping got roughly ten times cheaper. Deciding what
to build did not get cheaper, so it became the bottleneck, and the product side
got chatbots that draft and wait. Meanwhile nobody can answer why a call was
made without excavating a Slack thread from four months ago.

Supaprod runs the whole product lifecycle as one governed loop instead of six
disconnected tools.

It reads your user feedback, product data, competitors and market and tells you
what is worth building, with the evidence attached, and argues against the weak
bets before you commit. Then it writes the spec, builds it, and opens a real
pull request behind a merge gate no agent can cross. Then it checks what
actually shipped against the forecast that justified the decision, and re-ranks
what comes next from what it finds.

That third step is the one nobody else does, and it is why the system gets
sharper rather than just faster.
```

**2b. How did you arrive at this idea?**

```
I lived the problem for close to a decade, in three industries, and the job
underneath was identical every time: be the glue across a dozen tools, then
re-answer from memory why something was decided, months later, with the
evidence long buried.

I started at 21 building satellite communication systems at ISRO, India's
national space agency, for its Moon and Mars missions, where the hardware
launches once and there is no patch release. Then semiconductors at Infineon in
Munich. Then senior AI product manager at Intellect, on an AI platform that
200+ financial institutions across 70+ countries build on.

What made it urgent rather than merely annoying was watching agents arrive.
They do not fix this, they make it worse, because now a human answers for calls
they did not fully make.

I am also not alone in it. Product people at companies like OpenAI and DoorDash
are hand-building their own versions of this out of coding agents, MCP
connectors and memory files. One described spending 1,500 hours on her setup.
Nobody does that for a mild annoyance.
```

**2c. Who is your target audience?**

```
Land: solo founders and product managers on small teams. They feel this hardest,
they can start without procurement, and they are already paying for the problem
in tool sprawl.

Expand: product teams and enterprises, where the audit trail is the thing they
actually budget for. That budget exists today, split across a tracker, a docs
tool, a spec tool, a coding agent, and status meetings.

The sizing: TAM is over $300B a year, which is the product management work
budget rather than a software line item, roughly 2.6M product managers at about
$115K loaded cost. SAM is $2B growing to $12B. SOM is about $47M ARR.
```

**2d. Who are your competitors?**

```
My real competitor is the stitched stack. A product manager runs discovery in
one tool, writes the spec in a second, designs in a third, hands code to agents
in a fourth, ships behind flags in a fifth, and reads the result in a sixth.
Every one of those is excellent at its own step. The tax is not the tools, it
is the seams: context is re-entered at every handoff, and by the third one the
reasoning behind the call is gone.

Named and real: Samepage raised a $4.85M seed to surface signals for product
leaders. Brief captures decision context for agents. Productboard shipped
Spark. Notion launched Ship OS. Every one validates the loop and every one
stops one step short. They surface, draft, or dispatch. None checks the shipped
outcome against the decision that caused it and feeds that back.

That gap is structural, not a missing feature. Grading a decision needs the
call and its outcome inside one system, and a handoff is exactly where those
two get separated.

The second competitor is smaller and wins more deals: a folder of markdown
files. The argument for it is real, that low-level tools are more agent-friendly
because agents drive them better. Right about the premise, wrong about the
conclusion. An agent can write into a Notion page or a GitHub issue. It cannot
write a decision carrying its evidence, its author, a verdict slot and a human
gate into either. Low-level tools are agent-writable but not agent-governable,
and that seam opens the moment a second person or a fleet of agents touches the
work.
```

**3d. How are you leveraging AI?**

```
AI is not a feature in this product, it is the runtime. Three things are worth
naming to people who build.

One engine, one chokepoint. Every frontier model call in the product goes
through a single runtime chokepoint we own. A better model is a same-day
drop-in at zero engineering cost, so we do not race the models, we put them to
work. Your team also runs no second coding tool, because the build lane is
inside the product.

Earned autonomy rather than a permissions matrix. Agents graduate their own
permissions from their track record, the way you extend trust to a new
colleague rather than handing over the keys on day one, with floors on merge,
revert and delegate that nothing can override. There is an audit trail on every
agent action and one-key rollback on anything an agent produced.

An autonomous loop, not a chat box. A cron engine advances product missions
every minute through sense, decide, define, build, ship and learn. Agents open
real pull requests behind a merge gate no agent can cross.

And the company is built the way the product claims a company can be built.
I direct a fleet of agents and they write the code. No non-founder has touched
this codebase. Everything goes through typecheck, build and a review pass
before merge, and a separate reviewer independent of the agents that build
audits it for security.
```

```
Two reasons, and the second one is the honest gap rather than a compliment.

The first is who runs it. 886 Studios is built by people who shipped Twitch,
Kabam and Guitar Hero, which is operator experience in products where the loop
between a decision and its consequence is short and public. That is exactly the

The second is that I built the engine before opening the doors. For a product

I am leaving my job and building this full-time regardless of how any
```

**5c. Share an alternative idea you considered**

```
The alternative I took most seriously was building only the memory layer:
capture every product decision with its evidence, and sell the ability to
answer why we decided this.

I rejected it, and then in August I found out I had rejected it for the wrong
reason. I had assumed a stored record of decisions was itself defensible. A
full read of the market falsified that. Causes survive in Slack, email and call
recordings, and they have been reconstructed after the fact twice on the public
record, once by Vercel's COO rebuilding the reasoning behind a lost deal with
an agent he built in two days. So a pure memory product would have been a
feature waiting to be absorbed.

What survived the test is narrower. It is the forecast captured at decision
time: what a team believed would happen, recorded before the outcome was known.
That leaves no trace unless something captured it at the moment of the call, so
no volume of raw data reconstructs it. It also only exists if you are already
in the loop when the decision is made, which is why the product had to be the
whole lifecycle and not a filing cabinet bolted onto it.

The rejected idea is the reason the real one has the shape it has.
```

> This is the application's **one self-correction beat**, and 5c is the field that literally asks for it. It names what was believed, what refuted it, and what survived.
